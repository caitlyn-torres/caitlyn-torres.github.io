How do we take couples therapy from “oh gosh” to “aw”?

Actually is an online premarital counseling service. They wanted to reach more people with reliable mental health information and take the stigma out of relationship therapy.

My role
UX Researcher & Writer
Timeline
Nov 2021 to Mar 2022
Tools
Figma

The challenge

Actually had 14.4k Instagram followers but little website traffic. The brief was a quiz engaging enough to move people from social to the site, teach them something real about their relationship, and make therapy feel fun and approachable.

The solution

A gender-neutral personality quiz built on positive framing: it measures the habits therapists recommend, so every result is something you'd be glad to share.

View the prototype

Research

I ran stakeholder and subject-matter-expert interviews, a survey, in-depth user interviews, and heuristic and competitive analyses. Every number here comes from my own research with real participants. I grouped what they told me into themes using affinity mapping and a card sort. The audience is "the modern couple": working millennials in committed relationships. Actually takes a preventive approach, building communication and conflict-management skills early, before patterns of distress become entrenched.

34survey respondents in the target demographic
64.7%view couples counseling favorably
45.5%think it's only for when there are problems
86%like personality quizzes as an icebreaker

What I learned

There's still stigma

People like counseling in principle but read it as a sign something is wrong.

People love quizzes

Most enjoy personality tests whether or not they're scientific.

Men hesitate more

Men expressed more reluctance to seek counseling, in line with research on gender differences in help-seeking. Women described a partner's reluctance as a barrier to starting.

Design principles

Informative yet fun

Built on Gottman's Four Horsemen, with each negative pattern inverted into its positive antidote.

Shareable

People share what is useful, entertaining and emotionally positive, so results had to feel good.

Gender-neutral

No gendered language or imagery, to lower the barrier for men.

Testing changed the questions

First draftOh gosh. That's tough.

I watched for nonverbal cues as well as what people said. Testers scratched their heads and rubbed their foreheads: the questions asked about conflict and left them in a negative emotional state.

Later draftsAw, this is so sweet.

I reframed each question to measure the antidote instead of the problem, so the emotional response at the end was neutral or positive. It took three drafts of all ten questions. The design changed because of what I saw in the room.

Here is the same question across the three drafts. The wording moves from naming a problem to asking about a strength, and the answer choices change with it. By the final draft, every option is something positive a person could say about themselves, so there is no wrong answer to feel judged by.

Draft 1When you and your partner fight, does it ever lead to change?

Sample answer: “No, we just end up exhausted.”

Draft 2Do your arguments lead to solutions?

Sample answer: “We have the same fights over and over.”

FinalWhen faced with disagreements, what strategy do you find most effective?

Sample answer: “Taking a moment to listen and understand each other’s perspectives.”

Draft 1What’s the biggest problem in your relationship?

Sample answer: “I don’t know where to start.”

Draft 2What’s the biggest thing keeping you from improving your relationship?

Sample answer: “We are working on it, but progress is slow.”

FinalWhat motivates you to work through challenges in your relationship?

Sample answer: “A deep commitment to each other and the relationship.”

Outcome

37%increase in user engagement from the new flows and prototypes

How I work

A caller can't scan a voice menu the way they scan a screen, so everything has to fit in working memory. I keep prompts short, limit choices per turn, and map intents and entities to the words people actually use, so the system matches the caller's mental model and not the organization's.

Newer assistants generate their own wording, so more of my work is shaping how they behave: their tone, their scope and their limits. I read what they actually say and ask three things. Is it right? Did the person get what they came for? Did it feel appropriate? Whatever falls short goes back into the design.

I also design for failure: clarification, conversational repair and escalation, so an assistant is clear about its limits and hands off smoothly to a live agent. Healthcare and banking leave little room for a wrong answer, so I take extra care over what a customer could hear before anything goes live.

Voice and AI text-to-speech

In voice, how something is said is part of the design. Neural text-to-speech from tools like ElevenLabs has made synthetic voices sound far more natural, which raises the stakes for the script: pacing, pauses, emphasis and pronunciation all have to be written for the ear, and the voice has to fit the persona. A warmer voice also invites more trust, so I pay attention to keeping the assistant honest about being automated.

I've used ElevenLabs on Waterfield engagements in healthcare and retail, both to choose a voice that fits the assistant's persona and to tune pronunciation so that specialist terms and names come out right.

Spanish and inclusive design

I own all Spanish-language conversation content, with native-level translation and localization. I also apply linguistics and speech science to make conversations work for users with special needs.

A sample exchange

Thanks for calling the clinic. I'm the virtual assistant. How can I help today?

Open question, no menu. Says up front that it's automated.

Hi, I need to move my appointment. I can't do Thursday anymore.

No problem, let's move your Thursday appointment. So I can pull it up, what's your date of birth?

Confirms by repeating it back, keeps what the caller already said, and explains why it's asking.

March 3rd, 1988.

Thanks. I see your visit Thursday at 2:30. The next openings are Monday at 9 or Tuesday at 4. Would either work?

Two options at a time, so nothing has to be held in memory.

Is there anything later in the week?

Sure. Later in the week there's Friday at 1:15. Want that one?

Follows the caller's lead; doesn't repeat the same choices.

Wait, what time was Tuesday?

Tuesday was 4 PM. Would you rather have Tuesday at 4 or Friday at 1:15?

Repair: answers the question, then offers the choice again.

Friday.

Done. You're booked for Friday at 1:15, and Thursday is cancelled. I'll text you a confirmation. Anything else?

States the change plainly before closing. Asking for a person at any point reaches a live agent.

About

Good design is inclusive design. If it only works for some people, it isn't finished.

The tools I work with keep changing. What stays the same is paying close attention to the people on the other end and taking responsibility for what they experience. As a behavior therapist I collected behavioral data and shaped new habits one small, reinforced step at a time. I design the same way: map the user's mental model, reduce cognitive load, give clear feedback, and test with real people until the interaction feels natural for users of all abilities.

I help creative entrepreneurs and businesses use technology in ways that work for every user. With a background in behavior analysis and data management, I look at how people really interact with technology and where design can remove barriers. Accessibility is a fundamental part of my process from the first sketch.
